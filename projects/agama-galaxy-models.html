Equilibrium Galaxy
Modeling with AGAMA

Building equilibrium galaxy models with AGAMA's self-consistent modeling pipeline, then preparing them for large-scale N-body evolution with GADGET-4.

← Back to projects
01

It starts with a problem.

Before asking how a galaxy evolves, I wanted to ask a simpler question: can we build one that is already in equilibrium?

A galaxy is not just a collection of particles. Its stars move through a gravitational potential, continuously responding to the mass distribution around them. If the initial conditions are not self-consistent, an N-body simulation can spend much of its early evolution simply trying to settle itself.

So the goal was to construct the equilibrium first.

02

Finding the right language.

I used AGAMA and its self-consistent modeling pipeline to construct the system.

Underneath the software is a collection of ideas from galactic dynamics: the collisionless Boltzmann equation, action-angle variables, the Staeckel approximation, distribution functions, and multipole representations of the gravitational potential.

For the stellar components, I worked with quasi-isothermal and double-power-law distribution functions. The resulting density and potential are iteratively brought into agreement.

The interesting part is not generating particles.
It is making sure they belong to the galaxy you intended to build.
03

From equilibrium to particles.

Once the self-consistent model converges, the continuous distribution has to become something an N-body code can actually evolve.

I split the pipeline into two distinct stages.

01

Converge

run_iterate.py performs the self-consistent modeling stage, using OpenMP to accelerate the computation.

02

Sample

run_sample.py samples the converged distribution into particles and distributes the workload across MPI processes.

04

Then the scale changes.

A small equilibrium model is useful for understanding the physics. But the computational problem becomes very different when the system contains millions of particles.

The sampling stage was therefore designed around distributed memory rather than treating the entire particle population as a single in-memory object.

10⁷ particles

The target is the 10⁷-particle scale on a cluster.

The final particle realization can then be passed to GADGET-4, where the equilibrium model becomes the initial condition for a full N-body evolution.

05

What I am really exploring.

The project sits somewhere between astrophysics and computational science.

The physics determines what a valid galaxy looks like. The numerical methods determine whether we can construct it. And the architecture of the code determines how far we can scale it.

That intersection is what interests me most.

06

And this is only the beginning.

The next step is to follow these carefully constructed systems through time: measuring their stability, structure, and evolution under gravity.

Eventually, the same pipeline can become a starting point for more complicated galaxy models and larger cosmological simulations.

Build the equilibrium first.
Then see what happens when you let gravity take over.
