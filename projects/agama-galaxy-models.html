Equilibrium Galaxy Modeling with AGAMA

Building self-consistent galaxy models with AGAMA and preparing particle realizations for large-scale N-body evolution with GADGET-4.

01

Starting from equilibrium

When setting up an N-body simulation of a galaxy, the initial conditions matter as much as the integrator that evolves them. A system that is not dynamically consistent can immediately begin rearranging itself, producing transient behaviour that comes from the construction of the initial state rather than the physical question being studied.

I wanted to approach the problem from the other direction: instead of beginning with an arbitrary collection of particles, construct a galaxy whose density, gravitational potential, and phase-space distribution are mutually consistent.

This became the motivation for working with AGAMA.

02

Building the model

AGAMA provides a framework for constructing stellar systems through distribution functions and self-consistent gravitational potentials. Rather than prescribing the positions and velocities of every particle independently, the model begins with a description of the underlying phase-space distribution.

The important distinction is that the density distribution and the gravitational field are not treated as unrelated pieces. The mass distribution produces a potential, while the potential determines how the distribution function behaves.

The self-consistent modeling procedure repeatedly updates these components until the system converges toward a compatible density and potential.

This makes the resulting model substantially more useful as an N-body initial condition than simply placing particles into a visually plausible shape.

The interesting part is not generating particles. It is making sure they belong to the galaxy you intended to build.

03

From a distribution to particles

A continuous distribution function is useful for defining the equilibrium system, but eventually it has to become a finite set of particles that an N-body code can evolve.

I therefore think about the workflow as two connected computational stages.

01
Self-consistent modeling

Construct the density and gravitational potential, iterate the model, and bring the components toward equilibrium.

02
Particle sampling

Sample the converged distribution function into particles with positions and velocities suitable for subsequent N-body evolution.

03
Large-scale evolution

Pass the particle realization into a dedicated N-body framework such as GADGET-4 and follow the system through time.

04

Where the computational problem appears

Constructing a small model is only one part of the problem. The computational cost changes considerably when the goal becomes a high-resolution particle realization.

At that point, memory usage, particle generation, parallel execution, file formats, and communication between processes become part of the scientific problem itself.

The sampling stage is therefore being designed with distributed computation in mind rather than assuming that the entire particle population can exist comfortably in a single process.

10⁷ target particle scale

The longer-term target is to construct realizations at approximately the 10⁷-particle scale on cluster hardware and then use them as initial conditions for large N-body experiments.

05

From a static model to an evolving galaxy

An equilibrium model is not the final result. It is the starting point for asking what happens when the system is allowed to evolve under gravity.

Once the particle realization is passed to GADGET-4, the problem changes from constructing the initial state to measuring how that state responds over time.

This opens the possibility of studying structural evolution, stability, relaxation, morphology, and the response of different components of the galaxy to gravitational interactions.

The rendered simulations are therefore only the visible part of a much larger numerical pipeline.

06

Why I find the problem interesting

This project sits at an intersection I find particularly interesting: physical modeling, numerical methods, and high-performance computing.

The astrophysics tells us what a valid galaxy should look like. Numerical methods give us a way to construct and evolve it. Computational architecture determines how large the experiment can become.

Working on the same problem from all three directions has made the project as much about computational science as it is about galactic dynamics.

07

What comes next

The immediate goal is to make the complete pipeline robust: generate a self-consistent model, sample it efficiently, validate the resulting realization, and evolve it without introducing numerical artefacts that dominate the physical behaviour.

Beyond that, I want to move toward larger particle counts, more complex galaxy components, and eventually simulations where the initial equilibrium model becomes one part of a broader cosmological experiment.

The idea is simple, even if the computation is not: construct the system carefully enough that when gravity takes over, the interesting thing is the evolution itself.
