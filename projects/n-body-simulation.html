What happens
when gravity
is the only rule?

A from-scratch gravitational N-body simulation built to understand the mathematics, numerical methods and computational cost behind many-body gravitational systems.

01

It started with gravity.

I wanted to understand what actually happens underneath an N-body simulation rather than treating the solver as a black box.

At its core, the problem is deceptively simple: every body feels the gravitational force produced by every other body.

Fij = G mimj / rij2

From this single interaction comes a system whose behaviour can become surprisingly complicated as the number of bodies grows.

02

First, make two bodies behave.

Before dealing with many interacting particles, I started with the simplest system for which the dynamics are already known: two bodies orbiting their common centre of mass.

This became the first test of the numerical machinery. If the integrator cannot preserve a simple orbit, there is little reason to trust it when hundreds of bodies begin interacting.

03

Then time itself becomes numerical.

The equations of motion are continuous, but a computer has to advance the system in discrete time steps.

I implemented and compared several numerical approaches: Euler, Leapfrog/Verlet, fourth-order Runge–Kutta and Runge–Kutta–Fehlberg 45.

The comparison was not only about whether the particles followed approximately correct trajectories. I was interested in what happened to quantities that should remain conserved.

A simulation is only as useful as the physics and numerics hiding underneath it.

04

Trajectories are not enough.

A trajectory can look perfectly reasonable while the numerical solution slowly drifts away from the physical system it is supposed to represent.

So I tracked the quantities that should remain conserved: total energy and angular momentum.

These became a way of looking beyond the animation and asking whether the numerical method was actually behaving correctly.

05

Two bodies become many.

The interesting part begins when the system stops having a simple analytical picture.

With N bodies, every particle contributes to the gravitational field experienced by every other particle.

More particles do not just mean more particles. They mean more interactions.

For a direct pairwise calculation, the computational cost grows approximately as O(N²).

06

Then the computation becomes the problem.

The physics itself is compact. The number of interactions is not.

Every time step requires the gravitational influence of the surrounding particles to be evaluated. As N grows, the direct method quickly becomes dominated by these pairwise interactions.

This is where the project stops being only an astrophysics exercise and becomes a computational problem.

07

Building the machinery myself.

The project was structured so that the physical model, numerical integrators, energy calculations, stability checks and simulation driver could be developed independently.

08

The simulation is also data.

Each run produces a numerical record of the system rather than just an image on the screen.

I used structured simulation data to preserve particle states and make it possible to analyse the evolution after the run itself had finished.

This separation between simulation and analysis becomes increasingly important once the systems become too large to understand from an animation alone.

09

The next bottleneck is obvious.

Direct summation is useful because it makes the physics explicit, but it is not the final answer to the scaling problem.

The natural next step is to replace the direct O(N²) calculation with hierarchical methods such as the Barnes–Hut tree algorithm.

That would allow the same physical problem to be explored at particle counts where direct pairwise computation becomes increasingly expensive.

10

There is something satisfying about starting from zero.

No simulation engine was hiding the equations. No integrator was hiding the time evolution. No visualization library was deciding what the physics meant.

I wanted to understand the entire chain: from Newtonian gravity to numerical integration, from conservation laws to computational scaling.

Start with gravity.
Write down the equations.
Then let the particles move.
