What happens when gravity is the only rule?

A from-scratch gravitational N-body simulation built to understand the mathematics, numerical methods and computational cost behind many-body gravitational systems.

01

It started with gravity.

I wanted to understand what actually happens underneath an N-body simulation rather than treating the solver as a black box. At its core, the problem is deceptively simple: every body feels the gravitational force produced by every other body.

Fij = G(mimj) / rij2

From this single interaction comes a system whose behaviour can become surprisingly complicated as the number of bodies grows.

02

First, make two bodies behave.

Before dealing with many interacting particles, I started with the simplest system for which the dynamics are already known: two bodies orbiting their common centre of mass. This became the first test of the numerical machinery.

03

Then time itself becomes numerical.

The equations of motion are continuous, but a computer has to advance the system in discrete time steps. I implemented and compared several numerical approaches: Euler, Leapfrog/Verlet, fourth-order Runge–Kutta and Runge–Kutta–Fehlberg 45.

A simulation is only as useful as the physics and numerics hiding underneath it.

04

Trajectories are not enough.

A trajectory can look perfectly reasonable while the numerical solution slowly drifts away from the physical system it is supposed to represent. So I tracked the quantities that should remain conserved: total energy and angular momentum.

05

Two bodies become many.

The interesting part begins when the system stops having a simple analytical picture. With N bodies, every particle contributes to the gravitational field experienced by every other particle.

More particles do not just mean more particles. They mean more interactions. For a direct pairwise calculation, the computational cost grows approximately as O(N²).

06

Then the computation becomes the problem.

The physics itself is compact. The number of interactions is not. Every time step requires the gravitational influence of the surrounding particles to be evaluated. As N grows, the direct method quickly becomes dominated by these pairwise interactions.

07

Building the machinery myself.

The project was structured so that the physical model, numerical integrators, energy calculations, stability checks and simulation driver could be developed independently.

08 & 09

The simulation is also data, and the next bottleneck.

Each run produces a numerical record of the system rather than just an image on the screen. This separation between simulation and analysis becomes increasingly important once the systems become too large.

Direct summation is useful, but the natural next step is to replace the direct O(N²) calculation with hierarchical methods such as the Barnes–Hut tree algorithm.

10

There is something satisfying about starting from zero.

No simulation engine was hiding the equations. No integrator was hiding the time evolution. No visualization library was deciding what the physics meant.

Start with gravity. Write down the equations. Then let the particles move.
