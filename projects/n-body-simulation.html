01

Introduction

N-body simulations provide a numerical way to solve the equations of motion for systems of interacting particles. In astrophysics, they are used to investigate systems ranging from few-body and Solar System dynamics to galactic and cosmological scales.

This work compares a symplectic integration scheme, Velocity Verlet, with the non-symplectic Runge–Kutta methods RK4 and RK45. The main questions are how numerical accuracy, conservation of physical quantities, long-term stability, and computational cost change with the integration method and number of bodies.

The simulations numerically solve coupled equations of motion by discretizing time and approximating the trajectories of interacting bodies. This makes it possible to investigate orbital evolution, stability, and chaotic behaviour that becomes difficult to analyse analytically for systems with three or more bodies.

02

The N-body problem

Each body experiences a gravitational force from every other body. For the i-th body, Newton's second law gives:

mi d²ri/dt² = Fi

The total gravitational force is obtained by summing the contribution from all other bodies:

Fi = Σj ≠ i G mimj (rj − ri) / |rj − ri|³

Dividing by mi gives the acceleration used by the numerical integrators:

ai = Σj ≠ i G mj (rj − ri) / |rj − ri|³

The direct, or brute-force, method evaluates these interactions for every particle at every time step. The principal approximation is that the acceleration is treated as constant over the small interval Δt before being recomputed.

Physical assumptions

  • Newtonian gravity.
  • Point-like masses.
  • No relativistic corrections.
  • No external forces.
  • No non-gravitational interactions.
03

Numerical integration

Velocity Verlet

Velocity Verlet is a symplectic integration scheme particularly useful for long-term Hamiltonian dynamics. The method advances the velocity by half a time step, updates the position, recomputes the gravitational forces, and completes the velocity update.

RK4

The classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method evaluates intermediate slopes and combines them to obtain the next state. It provides high local accuracy when the dynamics are adequately resolved, but does not preserve the Hamiltonian structure of the gravitational system.

RK45

RK45 calculates fourth- and fifth-order approximations and estimates the local truncation error from their difference. The step can be accepted or rejected according to a chosen tolerance, allowing the step size to be controlled adaptively.

In the fixed-step comparisons of this work, RK45's adaptive capabilities provide little benefit unless adaptive stepping is explicitly enabled.

04

Physical diagnostics

Energy conservation

The total mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic and gravitational potential energy:

K(t) = Σi ½mi|vi(t)|²
U(t) = −Σi<j Gmimj / |ri(t) − rj(t)|
E(t) = K(t) + U(t)

Relative final energy drift is used as a principal numerical diagnostic:

ΔE/E = |E(T) − E0| / |E0|

Angular momentum

Angular momentum is monitored alongside energy to test whether the numerical evolution preserves another important conserved quantity of the isolated gravitational system.

ΔL/L = |L(T) − L0| / |L0|

Long-term stability

The comparison is not based only on whether an orbit looks correct. The simulations track how numerical errors accumulate over long integrations, making conservation diagnostics central to evaluating the integrators.

05

Results

The study examines systems ranging from simple two-body setups to simulations containing hundreds of particles. For two-body systems, all three integrators reproduce circular, elliptical, and parabolic orbital behaviour.

Two-body systems

Circular, elliptical, and parabolic trajectories are reproduced by all tested integrators.

Energy behaviour

Verlet produces bounded, oscillatory errors, while RK4 and RK45 show gradual long-term drift.

Scaling

Direct force evaluation gives the expected approximately quadratic O(N²) scaling.

As the number of bodies increases, the trajectories can remain visually similar while quantitative diagnostics reveal differences between the integration schemes.

Velocity Verlet maintains bounded errors in energy and angular momentum, characteristic of a symplectic method. RK4 and RK45 show gradual monotonic drift in conserved quantities over long integrations.

Smaller time steps improve the behaviour of all integrators, but the bounded-error behaviour of Verlet remains advantageous for long-term gravitational calculations.

Runtime measurements

The direct gravitational algorithm evaluates N(N − 1)/2 pairwise interactions per time step. Consequently, the computational cost grows approximately as N² for large N.

Implementation

The direct N-body algorithm initializes masses, positions, velocities, gravitational parameters, softening length, time step, final time, and the chosen numerical integrator.

INITIALIZE t ← 0 Store initial positions and velocities Compute initial energy E₀ Compute initial angular momentum L₀ WHILE t < T Compute gravitational forces FOR each body i Fᵢ ← 0 FOR each body j ≠ i rᵢⱼ ← rⱼ − rᵢ Compute softened gravitational force Fᵢ ← Fᵢ + Fᵢⱼ END FOR END FOR IF integrator = Verlet / Leapfrog Half-step velocity update Position update Recompute forces Complete velocity update END IF IF integrator = RK4 / RK45 Evaluate intermediate slopes Update positions and velocities END IF t ← t + Δt Compute E(t) and L(t) Store trajectories END WHILE POST-PROCESS Calculate energy drift Calculate angular-momentum drift Measure runtime Plot trajectories and diagnostics Compare drift and runtime against N

Softening

The implementation includes a softening length ε in the force calculation to reduce numerical problems associated with very small particle separations:

Fij = Gmimjrij / (|rij|² + ε²)3/2

Stored state

After integration, particle positions and velocities are stored for each time step. These trajectories can then be used independently for post-processing and scientific visualisation.

08

Discussion

The experiments show that choosing an integrator for gravitational N-body simulations involves more than comparing formal order or single-step accuracy.

For short, well-resolved integrations, the tested Runge–Kutta methods can reproduce the expected dynamics accurately. Over long time intervals, however, the preservation of conserved quantities becomes increasingly important.

Velocity Verlet's bounded and oscillatory energy and angular-momentum errors make it particularly suitable for long-term gravitational simulations. The runtime analysis also shows that force evaluation, rather than only the integrator's per-step overhead, dominates the computational cost as N grows.

The Solar System and 30-body simulations further demonstrate that the framework can represent both orbital mechanics and collective behaviour such as mass segregation.

09

Conclusion

A direct Newtonian N-body simulator was developed and applied to systems ranging from small particle numbers to hundreds of bodies, using Velocity Verlet, RK4, and RK45 integration schemes.

Runtime measurements confirm the expected O(N²) scaling of direct pairwise force evaluation. Energy diagnostics demonstrate the superior long-term stability of the symplectic Verlet scheme.

Overall, the study indicates that symplectic integration is well suited to long-term gravitational N-body calculations where preservation of energy and angular momentum is essential.

Future work

  • Barycentric Solar System simulations.
  • Adaptive symplectic integrators.
  • Tree-based force evaluation.
  • Simulations at significantly larger N.
