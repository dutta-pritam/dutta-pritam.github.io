Flight Computer & Avionics

2025–present, Embedded systems, Avionics

Hands-on experience building STM32-based flight computers and working on small-scale rocket motor development. But the journey here wasn't just about soldering PCBs—it was a trial by fire.

The Schoolyard Origins

Long before custom avionics and telemetry dashboards, this started as a high school obsession. We were mixing basic propellants, packing them into makeshift tubes, and praying they would go up instead of sideways. It was raw, scrappy engineering, but the thrill of seeing something you built break gravity for the first time was unparalleled.

Brain Power: The STM32 Era & Melagen Labs

As the airframes got bigger, the rockets started disappearing into the clouds. We needed data, and commercial altimeters were too limiting. I began developing custom flight computers based on the STM32 architecture to log altitude, acceleration, and actively deploy dual-stage recovery systems. The complexity of the embedded C code scaled with our ambitions.

That rigorous engineering caught the attention of Melagen Labs. Pitching our vision to them was a turning point. Their seed funding changed everything—we went from hardware store builds to precision-machined casings, aerospace-grade sensors, and proper data acquisition systems.

The "Room Bursting" Propulsion Anomaly

Of course, development isn't without its explosions. Propulsion is an unforgiving science. When pushing the limits of our new solid propellant mix, we ran a localized bench test to check ignition timings. We severely underestimated the exhaust velocity and chamber pressure.

The motor didn't just fire; it aggressively over-pressurized. The force blew the testing rig completely apart, shattered the room's glass fixtures, and covered every square inch of the lab in a thick layer of soot. It was a chaotic, heart-pounding day. But as we dug through the debris, we found the STM32 board intact. Analyzing the surviving telemetry taught us more about internal ballistics, thermal shock, and structural limits than any textbook ever could have.
